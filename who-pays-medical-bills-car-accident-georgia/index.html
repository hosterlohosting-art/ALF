GEORGIA LEGAL GUIDE • MEDICAL EXPENSES

Who Pays Medical Bills After a Car Accident in Georgia?

Medical bills can arrive long before a Georgia car accident claim is resolved. Ambulance care, emergency treatment, imaging, follow-up visits, therapy, medication, and specialist care may all generate separate charges. The practical question is not only who was at fault, but which coverage can pay while the claim is still being investigated.

Possible payment sources include health insurance, optional medical payments coverage, the at-fault driver's liability insurance through a settlement, and uninsured or underinsured motorist coverage. Which source applies depends on the policies, the people and vehicles involved, and the evidence.

Does the At-Fault Driver Pay Every Bill Immediately?

Usually, a bodily injury liability insurer evaluates medical expenses as part of the overall injury claim. It may not pay each provider as bills arrive. The insurer commonly investigates fault, causation, treatment, damages, and coverage before resolving the claim.

Georgia requires drivers to carry minimum liability limits of $25,000 per person and $50,000 per incident for bodily injury, according to the Georgia Office of Commissioner of Insurance. Those limits can be insufficient when injuries are serious or several people are hurt.

Health Insurance May Be the First Available Source

If you have health insurance, the medical provider may bill that plan while the auto claim is pending, subject to network rules, deductibles, copayments, coinsurance, exclusions, and plan terms. Using available health coverage can help prevent treatment from being delayed solely because liability has not been resolved.

A health plan may later assert reimbursement or subrogation rights against a settlement. Keep every explanation of benefits, bill, payment notice, and insurer letter so those issues can be reviewed before the claim closes.

Medical Payments Coverage (MedPay)

Medical payments coverage is an optional auto-insurance benefit in Georgia. Often called MedPay, it may pay covered medical expenses for an insured person or passenger up to the purchased limit, subject to the policy. Because it is contract-based, eligibility, covered people, deadlines, exclusions, and coordination with other insurance can vary.

Ask the auto insurer for the complete declarations page and policy, not only the insurance card. The Georgia insurance regulator advises consumers to know what their policy covers, excludes, and requires.

Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Coverage

If the responsible driver has no insurance or too little liability coverage, an applicable UM/UIM policy may provide another source of recovery. It is still necessary to prove fault, that the treatment was related to the crash, and the amount of the damages. Read our detailed Georgia uninsured motorist coverage guide.

What If You Do Not Have Health Insurance or MedPay?

Ask each provider about payment plans, financial-assistance programs, or other available billing options. Some providers may agree to defer collection under a written arrangement, but the terms should be read carefully. Do not assume a provider agreement guarantees that an insurer will pay the charge or that every billed amount will be recoverable.

Can Medical Bills Be Included in a Settlement?

Reasonable and necessary medical expenses caused by the collision may be presented as part of a bodily injury claim. Supporting evidence can include treatment records, itemized bills, diagnostic results, physician opinions, proof of payment, and evidence connecting the care to the crash.

The amount billed, the amount accepted by a provider, insurance adjustments, reimbursements, liens, and the amount ultimately owed may differ. A careful settlement review accounts for these differences before a release is signed.

Five Steps to Protect the Medical-Expense Claim

  1. Seek appropriate care promptly. Delayed treatment can harm health and create disputes about causation.
  2. Give providers accurate insurance information. Ask how each account is being billed.
  3. Save every document. Keep bills, records, receipts, explanations of benefits, prescription costs, and mileage notes.
  4. Notify applicable insurers. Georgia's insurance regulator recommends prompt notice even when another person caused the crash.
  5. Review reimbursement claims and releases. Confirm what must be repaid and what remains outstanding before settlement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I give the hospital the at-fault driver's insurance information?

You can provide accurate available information, but ask the billing department which coverage it will bill and what remains your responsibility. Keep written records of the answer.

Can an insurer reject medical treatment as unrelated?

An insurer may dispute causation, necessity, timing, or cost. Medical records, prior history, diagnostic evidence, and provider opinions can become important when responding.

Should I stop treatment because the other insurer has not paid?

Medical decisions should be made with qualified healthcare providers. Ask about available insurance and billing options rather than assuming the liability carrier will pay each bill immediately.
