Georgia Uninsured Motorist Claims: What UM/UIM Coverage Pays

A serious Georgia car accident becomes more complicated when the responsible driver has no insurance or not enough coverage. Uninsured motorist and underinsured motorist coverage—usually shortened to UM/UIM—may allow an injured person to seek compensation through an applicable auto policy instead of relying only on the at-fault driver’s assets.

Georgia’s Office of Commissioner of Insurance explains that uninsured motorist coverage protects policyholders when an uninsured driver causes bodily injury or property damage. Whether a particular loss is covered, how much is available, and which policies apply depend on the policy language and facts of the collision.

Uninsured Versus Underinsured Motorist Claims

An uninsured motorist claim may arise when the at-fault driver has no valid liability insurance. It may also become relevant in a hit-and-run when the driver cannot be identified, subject to the policy and Georgia’s proof requirements. An underinsured motorist claim may arise when the other driver has insurance, but the available liability limit is not enough to cover the documented losses.

Georgia requires minimum auto liability limits of $25,000 per person and $50,000 per incident for bodily injury, plus $25,000 for property damage. Those limits can be exhausted quickly by emergency care, surgery, lost income, or injuries to several people. Learn how insurance limits fit into the broader calculation in our guide to what a Georgia accident claim may be worth.

What UM/UIM Coverage May Compensate

Depending on the policy and the proven loss, a UM/UIM claim may seek compensation for medical expenses, future treatment, lost wages, reduced earning capacity, pain and suffering, and covered property damage. Coverage is not automatic payment: the claimant still generally must establish fault, causation, damages, and compliance with the policy.

Five Steps That Help Protect the Claim

  1. Report the crash and call law enforcement. Obtain the incident or police report number and preserve the other driver’s information.
  2. Notify the appropriate insurers promptly. A delayed notice can create a coverage dispute. Give accurate facts without guessing about injuries or fault.
  3. Document every available policy. Coverage may exist through the vehicle, a resident relative, an employer, or another policy, depending on the circumstances.
  4. Preserve proof. Keep photographs, witness details, medical records, bills, wage documents, repair estimates, and every insurer letter. Our Georgia crash-report guide explains how to secure the official report.
  5. Do not sign a release too early. A liability settlement or coverage document can affect remaining claims. Have it reviewed before signing.

Why Your Own Insurer May Still Dispute the Claim

A UM/UIM carrier can investigate fault, question whether treatment was related to the crash, challenge the value of damages, or dispute whether the policy applies. Treat the claim as an evidence-based process. Keep communications organized and request that any denial identify the policy provision relied upon. Our guide to insurance adjuster tactics explains common pressure points.

How Multiple Policies and Coverage Types Matter

Some Georgia policies provide “added-on” uninsured motorist coverage, while others provide “reduced-by” coverage. The distinction can change the amount potentially available after the at-fault driver’s liability coverage is credited. Multiple vehicles or policies can add another layer of analysis. Do not assume the declarations page alone answers every coverage question; endorsements and exclusions matter too.

Deadlines and Lawsuit Procedure

Insurance notice requirements and legal filing deadlines are separate issues. Georgia injury claims are often governed by a two-year limitation period, but exceptions and special circumstances can change the analysis. Review our Georgia statute of limitations guide and seek individualized advice promptly.
